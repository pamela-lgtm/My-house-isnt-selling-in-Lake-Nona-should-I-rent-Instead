Should You Sell or Rent Your Orlando Property in 2026?

For Lake Nona owners, the answer is not hidden in a headline. It is found in the property’s realistic sale proceeds, achievable rent, carrying costs, condition and your time horizon.

By Pamela Syvertson, Owner & Broker · Updated August 28, 2026

The market is not collapsing. It has become far more selective—on both the sales and rental sides.

Many Orlando owners are caught between two imperfect choices: accept a softer sales result today, or become a landlord in a rental market that also demands realistic pricing and professional execution.

What the Orlando sales data is actually telling us

In July 2026, Orlando’s median home price eased from $416,308 to $410,494, while monthly sales declined 7.4%. Homes averaged 64 days on market and the region carried 4.4 months of supply. The Orlando Regional REALTOR® Association also reported that 68% of surveyed local agents were seeing more seller concessions than a year earlier.

Buyer affordability remains central. Sixty-four percent of surveyed agents said buyers were waiting for lower rates, while 53% said buyers were shopping at lower price points to keep payments manageable. The average 30-year mortgage rate was 6.66% on August 27, 2026.

The practical meaning:
A seller can still succeed, but an aspirational asking price is not the same thing as a probable closing price. The useful number is the owner’s expected net after concessions, repairs, commissions and carrying costs.

Renting is an alternative—not an automatic answer

Rental demand remains meaningful in Lake Nona, supported by employment, relocations and the area’s long-term desirability. But rental owners face growing competition from professionally operated apartment and build-to-rent communities, many offering modern amenities and leasing incentives.

As of August 2026, RentCafe reported that average Lake Nona apartment rents were 3.44% lower than a year earlier. Its three-bedroom apartment average was $2,551. Those figures cover larger apartment properties and should not be used as direct valuations for individual houses or townhomes. They are, however, an important competitive signal: renters have choices.

A well-located, show-ready home that is properly priced can still perform. A dated or overpriced property may sit vacant—just as an overpriced sales listing may sit unsold.

The vacancy calculation every owner should understand

Suppose the market supports rent of $3,100, but the property is listed at $3,300 and loses one additional month to vacancy. The owner gives up approximately $3,100 in rent to pursue an extra $200 per month. It takes more than 15 months to recover that one-month loss—even before counting utilities, lawn care and other vacancy expenses.

When selling may be the more responsible choice

  • You need liquidity or a predictable exit date.
  • The realistic rental produces substantial negative monthly cash flow.
  • Major capital expenses are approaching and you do not want additional exposure.
  • Your likely holding period is short.
  • The HOA, property condition or insurance situation makes renting impractical.

When renting and holding may deserve serious consideration

  • You have attractive existing financing and can tolerate normal market fluctuations.
  • The home is in a durable rental location and can compete on condition.
  • You do not need immediate sale proceeds.
  • You have a multi-year investment horizon.
  • You are prepared to budget for vacancy, maintenance and capital reserves.

Do not turn an unsuccessful sales listing into an unexamined rental

A property that did not sell should not simply be moved into the rental market with the same assumptions. It needs a rental analysis, rent-readiness review, HOA and lease-term verification, insurance review, competitive positioning and a realistic reserve plan.

At Verandah, we return to three fundamentals: location, condition and price. Location is fixed. Condition is controlled by the owner. Price must respond to actual market feedback. Professional management cannot repeal the market, but it can help an owner interpret it early and act before vacancy or deferred decisions become unnecessarily expensive.

The decision should be made with property-specific numbers

A sound comparison includes expected net sale proceeds, realistic rent, mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA expenses, management, maintenance reserves, vacancy exposure and the owner’s intended holding period. Potential tax consequences should be reviewed with the owner’s qualified tax adviser.
